How drug court works

Supervised treatment instead of a prison sentence.

What the program asks of people, and why it works better than incarceration.

The basic idea

A person facing certain non-violent charges, whose offense is driven by a substance use disorder, may be referred to drug court instead of moving through traditional case processing. Rather than a cell, they get a treatment plan and a judge who watches their progress closely.

The commitment is real: a minimum of one year, and most people find it harder than the sentence they would otherwise have served. That is the point. Drug courts keep people in treatment long enough for treatment to actually work.

  1. Intensive treatment and support

    Participants receive the treatment and services they need to reach and sustain recovery — clinical care, counselling, and the practical supports around it.

  2. Accountability to the judge

    Participants are held accountable by the drug court judge for meeting their obligations to the court, to the community, to their families, and to themselves.

  3. Regular, random drug testing

    Testing is frequent and unannounced. It is what makes the accountability meaningful rather than theoretical.

  4. Frequent court appearances

    Participants appear in court often so the judge can review progress directly, in person, rather than through paperwork.

  5. Incentives and sanctions

    Doing well is rewarded. Falling short brings a graduated sanction. The Foundation funds many of the incentives — the small, concrete recognitions that make progress visible.

The evidence

Why courts and county commissions keep funding this.

  • Drug courts reduce substance use.
  • Drug courts reduce crime.
  • Drug courts save public money.
  • Drug courts restore lives.
  • Drug courts keep families together and reunite children with parents.

Drug courts currently reach only a fraction of the people in the justice system living with a substance use disorder. Breaking the cycle of substance use and crime means putting a drug court within reach of everyone who needs one — including here.

Common questions

Who is eligible for drug court in Flagler County?

[CLIENT / COURT TO CONFIRM] Eligibility is set by the Seventh Judicial Circuit, not by the Foundation. We need the current criteria — charge types, prior record limits, residency, and the referral route — before publishing anything here, because a wrong answer could keep someone from applying.

How does someone get referred?

[CLIENT / COURT TO CONFIRM] Who initiates a referral — defense counsel, the State Attorney, the judge, or self-referral — and the contact for the drug court coordinator.

How long does the program take?

A minimum of one year. The actual length depends on the person's progress through the program's phases.

What does the Foundation pay for?

We fund the things the court budget does not cover: incentives that recognize progress, transportation to treatment and court, testing costs, graduation, free Narcan, and community training. See Ways to Give.

Is the Foundation part of the court?

No. We are an independent 501(c)(3) nonprofit (EIN 27-1349987) that supports the program. Having a nonprofit backing a public agency is unusual — it is what lets the program act quickly without waiting on a budget cycle.

Help fund the parts the court can't.

A ride, a test, a kit, a graduation. Small things, at the exact moment they matter.

Need help now?

  • 988 — Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. Call or text, 24/7.
  • 1-800-662-4357 — SAMHSA National Helpline. Free, confidential treatment referrals, 24/7.
  • 211 — Florida 211. Local treatment, housing, and food assistance.
  • If someone is overdosing right now, call 911. Florida's Good Samaritan law protects people who call for help.
